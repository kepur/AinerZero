 Abstract 
 Coupled systems of ordinary differential equations (ODEs) are widely used to model complex physical and biological processes. In these applications, ODE model parameters must be calibrated to field data to enable prediction and parameter inference. In practice, the governing ODEs are an imperfect approximation to the true system, leading to non-negligible model discrepancy that must be incorporated to avoid biased parameter estimates. However, it is well known that highly flexible discrepancy models can confound discrepancy and simulator parameters, leading to poor identifiability. As a result, practitioners often must impose problem-specific prior constraints to adequately regularize the discrepancy, which can be challenging and cumbersome. In this work, we propose a novel calibration framework for coupled, multi-output ODE systems that enforces automatic, model-structural constraints on the discrepancy, improving identifiability without requiring application-specific discrepancy priors. Forward-model gradients are computed via sensitivity equations, solved jointly with the ODE system as a coupled initial value problem. Posterior inference is performed using an adaptive Metropolis-within-Gibbs sampler tailored to the resulting constrained posterior. We demonstrate the approach on three model systems: a mass-spring oscillator, a model of infectious disease spread, and a neuron firing model. We show that our method leads to improved parameter inference and predictive accuracy compared to standard calibration approaches. 
 1 Introduction 
 Model calibration is a necessary component in how simulation models are used for real-world decision making [ 27 , 35 ] . The integration of data with a computer model, often called the inverse problem , leads to a data-specific simulator that can be used for understanding an underlying process. In the case of mechanistic models, such as systems of differential equations, the model equations themselves embed hypotheses about the processes observed, and are almost always parameterized by interpretable variables that may or may not be measured [ 10 , 35 ] . These simulation platforms form an important foundation for emerging digital twin technologies [ 27 ] , as mechanistic models provide a way of forecasting the behavior of the system while being continually informed or updated by data. 
 Accounting for model discrepancy , defined as the systematic difference between the true physical process and its simulator, is critical when calibrating differential-equation-based models to ensure reliable inference, prediction, and uncertainty quantification (UQ) [ 6 ] .
To address this important aspect of model calibration, in this work we investigate both parameter and predictive inference in the calibration of multi-output ordinary differential equation (ODE) models from noisy data under model discrepancy. Specifically, we consider data arising from a statistical model of the form 
 $\displaystyle y_{i}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\xi(t_{i})+\epsilon_{i}$ 
 (1) 
 $\displaystyle=Az(t_{i},\theta)+\delta_{\theta}(t_{i})+\epsilon_{i};\quad i=1,...,n.$ 
 In eq. ( 1 ), $A:\mathbb{R}^{J\times K}$ is a linear measurement operator, $t_{i}\in\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}$ are observation times, $\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}$ is the unknown true calibration parameter, $\xi(\cdot)$ is the unknown true physical reality, and $z(t_{i},\theta):\Omega\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{K}$ is the forward model at some $t_{i}$ . The term $\delta_{\theta}(t_{i})=\xi(t)-z(t_{i},\theta)$ is the model discrepancy and $\epsilon_{i}$ is a mean-zero measurement error process. The matrix $A:\mathbb{R}^{K}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{J}$ maps the full state vector $z(t_{i},\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$ to the observable state space $\mathbb{R}^{J}$ . Given noisy observations $\mathcal{D}:=\{(t_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}$ from the physical process, the primary goals are (i) to infer $\theta$ and quantify its uncertainty and (ii) to improve predictions of the physical process while quantifying their uncertainty. 
 The contribution of this work is a novel calibration framework that extends the popular class of orthogonal Gaussian process discrepancy priors [ 32 ] from univariate calibration to the calibration of mulit-output ODE simulators using multivariate observations. They key to our approach is the development of a multivariate discrepancy prior which is orthogonal to the forward model sensitives. We develop a complete analytical framework and scalable computational inference algorithm based on model sensitivity equations for our multivariate orthogonal sensitivity (MOS) approach and apply it to several model types. We show that the MOS approach performs as well or better than the standard approach in both estimation and UQ. 
 The outline of the article is as follows. In Section 2 we provide a brief overview of the general problem of calibration when model discrepancy is present, along with a review of calibration and sensitivity systems for ODE systems. Section 3 provides the mathematical preliminaries and derivation of the multivariate orthogonal discrepancy prior, as well as a computational inference algorithm for sampling from the relevant Bayesian posteriors. Section 4 provides numerical details for our case studies, as well as three prototypical ODE system models: a mass-spring-damper system, the Fitzhu-Nagumo model of neuron activity, and a “susceptible-infected-recovered-vaccinated” (SIRV) model. Here, we investigate how our proposed MOS method compares to classical multivariate calibration approaches in both parameter and prediction metrics. We provide a discussion and conclusion in Section 5 . 
 2 Background 
 2.1 Calibration with Model Discrepancy 
 Eq. ( 1 ) is a special case of the calibration problem under model discrepancy. In the univariate output ( $J=1$ ) setting, parameter inference and prediction for this model have received extensive treatment across the Bayesian [ 6 , 8 , 23 , 32 , 44 ] , frequentist [ 38 , 42 ] , and machine learning [ 4 , 21 , 33 , 40 ] literature. The influential approach by Kennedy and O’Hagan (KOH) [ 23 ] propose the use of a flexible Gaussian process prior on $\delta$ . It is well known that this approach can suffer from identifiability issues between discrepancy and calibration parameters unless additional prior information is used to further constrain the prior on $\delta$ [ 6 ] .
A variety of remedies to this identifiability issue have been proposed [ 2 , 36 ] . $L^{2}$ -calibration [ 38 ] addresses the issue by redefining the true $\theta$ as an $L^{2}$ minimizer between the physical process and the forward model,
and propose an asymptotically consistent frequentist calibration procedure. Bayesian approaches constrain the discrepancy prior: [ 14 ] encourages identifiability by imposing a prior to promote smaller (in $\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}$ norm) discrepancies, while [ 32 ] use the first order conditions implied by $L^{2}$ optimality to linearly constrain a Gaussian process prior for $\delta_{\theta}$ . 
 The $J\geq 2$ multivariate setting has garnered less attention. A common approach extends KOH by using independent multivariate Gaussian process priors [ 18 , 29 ] .
However, this unconstrained approach can inherit similar or worse identifiability issues as outlined above in the univariate case. Constrained priors for $\delta_{\theta}$ in the multivariate setting are seemingly limited, though a recent work [ 7 ] proposes a framework based on posterior projections [ 1 ] , while [ 41 ] use PCA in the output space to map into a low-rank subspace, in which a simplified bi-linear model, motivated by identifiability considerations, is proposed for the discrepancy in the reduced space. A very recent study by Barnett and Chan [ 3 ] extended the $L^{2}$ orthogonality criteria from [ 32 ] to the multioutput setting. This last example considered discrepancy between Gaussian process surrogates and the true simulation model, but did not extend this to handing discrepancy between simulators and observations. 
 2.2 Calibration for Multivariate ODE Systems 
 We consider the case in which the state variables $z(t_{i},\theta)$ in eq. ( 1 ) evolve according to a system of ODEs. Systems of ODEs are a common modeling tool to describe the evolution of physical and biophysical processes [ 10 ] . The broad definition of the system is given by 
 $\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}z(t,\theta)=G(t,z(t,\theta),\theta),\quad z(0)=z_{0},$ 
 (2) 
 with $z(t,\theta):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}$ being the state variables which evolve in time according to some evolution equation $G(t,z(t,\theta),\theta)$ from initial state $z_{0}$ , and $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}$ is the temporal domain. In the context of ODEs, the calibration problem in eq. ( 1 ) now defines $Az(t_{i},\theta)$ to be a mapping from the full states of the ODE system to the observations. This requires an additional level of care, as the solutions corresponding to observations are now approximations provided by numerical integration (e.g., using Runge-Kutta methods) at discrete time points for a possibly smaller subset of state variables (e.g., if $A$ is sparse). 
 The most naive approach to solving these inverse problems is to (i) choose a current value for $\theta$ , (ii) numerically integrate the ODE system between $p+1$ and $2p+1$ times for a gradient approximation, and (iii) calculate a search direction by evaluating the residual between the observations and the subset of state solutions. This can become computationally expensive if either the ODE system is stiff, requiring small time-stepping, or if the dimensionality of the model is large, requiring repeated numerical integration in order to calculate the $p$ dimensional gradient of the objective function. 
 Multiple prior studies across scientific domains have considered inverse problems governed by ODE systems. For instance, ODE models of temperature in air-cycle machines can be used for monitoring heat transfer in applications relevant to aerospace engineering [ 28 ] . Epidemiological applications are especially well suited for using ODE models. Numerous applications have looked at simulating the transmission of disease from those who are susceptible to an infected and recovered state, often called the SIR model [ 37 , 39 ] . In neuroscience, models of neuronal excitation can be described through coupled ODE systems, where ion channels on a neuron can lead to excitatory and inhibitory interactions [ 13 , 22 ] . While high-fidelity data on neuronal firing in rare, many groups use such ODE models to simulate whole-brain electrical activity as measured through imaging modalities [ 11 ] . Finally, the cardiovascular field has extensively used ODE models with available clinical data for calibration [ 9 , 25 , 34 ] . These models include electrophysiological dynamics at the cell-level as well as compartment, organ-level models for simulating blood flow and overall cardiovascular function. 
 2.3 Sensitivity Systems 
 An alternative approach to approximating the gradient is to derive the so-called “sensitivity equations” to provide the Jacobian of the system analytically [ 45 ] . Consider the general form of the sensitivity of state $z_{j}(t,\theta)$ with respect to parameter $\theta_{k}$ : 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{jk}(t)=\frac{\partial z_{j}(t)}{\partial\theta_{k}}.$ 
 (3) 
 Note that the sensitivities evolve in time with the dynamics of $z(t,\theta)$ , and the total derivative of the differentiation equation with respect to $\theta$ can be written as 
 $\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\theta}\frac{dz}{dt}=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial z}{\partial\theta}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathcal{S}}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}G(t,z(t,\theta),\theta)$ 
 (4) 
 $\displaystyle\implies\frac{d\mathcal{S}}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{\partial G}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial\theta}+\frac{\partial G}{\partial\theta}=\mathcal{G}_{z}\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{G}_{\theta}$ 
 (5) 
 where $\partial G/\partial z=\mathcal{G}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{(p\ \cdot K)\times(p\ \cdot K)}$ is a block diagonal system with the state-Jacobian, $\mathcal{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times K}$ , of the system on the diagonal, $\mathcal{S}$ is the sensitivity of the states with respect to the parameters, and $\partial G/\partial\theta=\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\ \cdot K}$ is the parameter-Jacobian, with derivatives of the right hand side with respect to $\theta$ . We note that typically the sensitivity is written in matrix form with $\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times p},\mathcal{G}_{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times K},$ and $\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times P}$ , but here we define everything in vectorized form for a cleaner translation to the available codes. 
 If the initial conditions of the ODE system are known, as will be assumed here, then $\mathcal{S}(0)=0$ . Then, we can write the sensitivity in combination with the original ODE system as a coupled initial value problem: 
 $\displaystyle\frac{dz}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=G(t,z(t,\theta),\theta),\ \ \ z(0)=z_{0}$ 
 (6) 
 $\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{S}}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mathcal{G}_{z}\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{G}_{\theta},\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \mathcal{S}(0)=0$ 
 (7) 
 on the time interval $\Omega$ . 
 Sensitivity systems have been the focus of some modeling studies, though typically these sensitivities are numerically approximated using finite differences. Some of the theoretical foundations for this approach were provided by seminal works by Leis et. al [ 26 ] and Dickison et. al [ 12 ] who derived such a sensitvity system for ordinary differential equation models. The review by Zi [ 45 ] provides an excellent overview of how such sensitivity systems can be developed in biological modeling applications. Wu et al. [ 43 ] provides examples of this in the context of biochemical processes, while work by Guay et. al [ 15 ] apply this to chemical kinetic systems. 
 3 Inference Methodology 
 3.1 Inferential Target and Observation Model 
 We assume that the sensitivity system defined in eq. ( 7 ) is well defined, smooth, and continuous [ 45 ] . We work in the inner product space induced by the direct sum of component-wise $L^{2}$ spaces $\mathcal{H}:=\bigtimes_{j=1}^{J}L^{2}(\Omega)$ , with inner product defined as 
 $\langle v,u\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}\langle v_{j},u_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)},$ 
 (8) 
 where $\alpha_{j}\geq 0$ are optional weights. With loss of generality, for clarity of notation, from here on we will assume $\alpha_{j}=1$ $\forall j$ . Clearly, $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}$ induces a norm, which we will denote as $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}$ . We define our inferential target using the $L^{2}$ calibration framework [ 38 ] , suitably extended to the multivariate setting using the loss induced by the norm $\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}$ to define the minimizer: 
 $\theta:=\min_{\eta\in\Theta}\left\|\xi(\cdot)-Az(\cdot,\eta)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2},$ 
 (9) 
 where we assume $\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}$ is compact. 
 The observed data under model eq. ( 1 ) with $J\geq 2$ is collected into the matrix $Y\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times n}$ . We define the matrix of forward model and discrepancy evaluations at the fixed time points $t_{1},...,t_{n}\in\Omega$ as $Z_{\theta},\Delta_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times n}$ , respectively, with element-wise definitions $[Z_{\theta}]_{ji}=[Az]_{j}(x_{i},\theta)$ and $[\Delta_{\theta}]_{ji}=\delta_{j}(x_{i};\theta)$ . We assume normality and homoscedasticity on the additive measurement errors with variance $\sigma^{2}$ and define the $J\times n$ matrix-valued random variable $E\sim\mathcal{MN}_{J\times n}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{J},I_{n})$ , where
 $\mathcal{MN}$ denotes the matrix normal distribution. Thus, in matrix form ( 1 ) becomes 
 $Y=Z_{\theta}+\Delta_{\theta}+E$ 
 (10) 
 where, with slight abuse of notation, $Z_{\theta}$ incorporates the linear measurement operator. Taking a Bayesian approach, we are interested in sampling the marginal posterior density over the calibration parameter: 
 $\pi(\theta|Y)\propto\int\pi(Y|\Delta_{\theta},\theta)\pi(\Delta_{\theta}|\theta)\pi(\theta)d\Delta_{\theta}.$ 
 (11) 
 Given the observation model eq. ( 10 ), it follows that $\pi(Y|\Delta_{\theta},\theta)$ is a matrix normal density while $\pi(\theta)$ is the parameter prior density determined by problem specific information, e.g. uniform density over parameter bounds. In the following subsection, we propose a multivariate analog to the orthogonal discrepancy prior from [ 32 ] to construct the prior density $\pi(\Delta_{\theta}|\theta)$ based on the structural constraints imposed by Equation eq. ( 9 ). 
 3.2 Multivariate Orthogonal Discrepancy Prior 
 We begin with the following lemma, which characterizes a collection of $p$ linear constraints for which the true multivariate discrepancy must obey under the definition of the optimal calibration in Equation  9 . 
 Lemma 1 . 
 Denote $\mathcal{S}(t,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times p}$ be the point-wise sensitivity of forward model $z(t,\eta)$ , with element-wise definition $[\mathcal{S}(t,\eta)]_{jp}=\frac{\partial z_{j}(t,\eta)}{\partial\eta_{p}}$ . Provided $\theta$ is in the interior of $\Theta$ , the first order optimality conditions of $\theta$ from eq. ( 9 ) imply 
 $\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{S}(t,\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}\delta_{\theta}(t)dt=0,\quad 0\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$ 
 (12) 
 Given these a-priori structural constraints, we desire a discrepancy prior such that the realization satisfy eq. ( 12 ) with probability one. The following result, which is a multivariate extension of Theorem 3 from [ 32 ] , provides a general construction for such a prior. 
 Theorem 1 . 
 Let $C_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})$ be a symmetric cross-variance function for a $J$ -multivariate process with q-dimensional hyperparameters $\phi$ . Let 
 $\displaystyle h_{\theta}(t)$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{S}(t^{\prime},\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}C_{\phi}(t^{\prime},t)dt^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times J}$ 
 (13) 
 $\displaystyle H_{\theta}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{S}(t^{\prime},\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}C_{\phi}(t^{\prime},t)A\mathcal{S}(t,\theta)dtdt^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}$ 
 and define the covariance function 
 $C_{\theta,\phi}(t,t^{\prime})=C_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})-h_{\theta}^{\intercal}(t)H_{\theta}^{-1}h_{\theta}(t^{\prime}),$ 
 (14) 
 Then for $\delta_{\theta}\sim GP(0,C_{\theta,\phi})$ , eq. ( 14 ) holds with probability one. 
 The quantities in eq. ( 13 ) require the evaluation of the Jacobian of the ODE system $z(\cdot,\theta)$ with respect to $\theta$ , which can be done jointly with a forward pass of the model by solving the coupled sensitivity ODEs ( 6 ) and ( 7 ) to get $\mathcal{S}$ . 
 3.3 Posterior Inference 
 Given the data and prior models outlined in Section  3.1 and Section  3.2 , respectively, our primary goal is to perform posterior inference for estimation and uncertainty quantification on $\theta$ . We are also interested in inferring the model discrepancy. 
 We begin by considering the calibration parameter posterior by calculating the marginalization integral eq. ( 11 ) under the discrepancy prior outlined in Section  3.2 . Let vec denote the vectorization operator and define the vectorized variables $\vec{y}:=\text{vec}(Y)$ , $\vec{z}_{\theta}:=\text{vec}(Z_{\theta})$ and $\vec{\delta}_{\theta}:=\text{vec}(\Delta_{\theta})$ , where the stacking is performed columnwise, that is, 
 $\text{vec}(\Delta_{\theta})=\left(\vec{\delta}_{1},\vec{\delta}_{2},...,\vec{\delta}_{n}\right)^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{Jn},$ 
 where $\vec{\delta}_{m}=(\delta_{1}(t_{m};\theta),...,\delta_{J}(t_{m};\theta))\in\mathbb{R}^{J}$ . Under the multivariate mean-zero GP prior with covariance function eq. ( 14 ), we have 
 $\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left[\vec{\delta}_{\theta}\right]$ 
 $\displaystyle:=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\delta}_{1}\vec{\delta}_{1}^{\intercal}\right]&\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\delta}_{1}\vec{\delta}_{2}^{\intercal}\right]&\cdots&\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\delta}_{1}\vec{\delta}_{n}^{\intercal}\right]\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\delta}_{2}\vec{\delta}_{1}^{\intercal}\right]&\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\delta}_{2}\vec{\delta}_{2}^{\intercal}\right]&\cdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\delta}_{n}\vec{\delta}_{1}^{\intercal}\right]&\cdots&&\mathbb{E}\left[\vec{\delta}_{n}\vec{\delta}_{n}^{\intercal}\right]\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{\theta,\phi}(t_{1},t_{1})&C_{\theta,\phi}(t_{1},t_{2})&\cdots&C_{\theta,\phi}(t_{1},t_{n})\\
C_{\theta,\phi}(t_{2},t_{1})&C_{\theta,\phi}(t_{2},t_{2})&\cdots&\vdots\\
\vdots&&\ddots\\
C_{\theta,\phi}(t_{n},t_{1})&\cdots&&C_{\theta,\phi}(t_{n},t_{n})\end{pmatrix}.$ 
 (15) 
 Let $\vec{t}=(t_{1},...,t_{n})$ and
with slight abuse of notation, we denote $\text{Cov}\left[\vec{\delta}_{\theta}\right]:=C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{Jn\times J_{n}}$ 
and hence $\vec{\delta}_{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}_{Jn}(0,C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t}))$ . 
 From the linear model eq. ( 10 ) we have that $Y|Z_{\theta},\Delta_{\theta}$ is a matrix normal random variable: $\mathcal{MN}_{J\times n}(Z_{\theta}+\Delta_{\theta},\sigma^{2}I_{J},I_{n})$ , and hence by properties of the matrix-normal we have the vectorized likelihood 
 $\pi(\vec{y}|z_{\theta},\delta_{\theta},\sigma^{2})=\mathcal{N}_{Jn}(\vec{y};\vec{z}_{\theta}+\vec{\delta}_{\theta},\sigma^{2}I_{nJ}).$ 
 (16) 
 Putting these identities together, we can write re-write the posterior of interest eq. ( 11 ) in terms of the vectorized distributions as 
 $\displaystyle\pi(\theta|\vec{y},\sigma^{2},\phi)$ 
 $\displaystyle\propto\int\mathcal{N}_{Jn}(\vec{y};\vec{z}_{\theta}+\vec{\delta}_{\theta},\sigma^{2}I_{nJ})\mathcal{N}_{Jn}(\delta_{\theta};\vec{0},C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t}))\pi(\theta)d\vec{\delta}_{\theta}$ 
 (17) 
 $\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{N}_{Jn}(\vec{y};\vec{z}_{\theta},C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t})+\sigma^{2}I_{nJ})\pi(\theta)$ 
 $\displaystyle\propto\frac{\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\vec{y}-\vec{z}_{\theta}\right)^{\intercal}\left(C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t})+\sigma^{2}I_{nJ}\right)^{-1}\left(\vec{y}-\vec{z}_{\theta}\right)\right)\pi(\theta)}{\det|C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t})+\sigma^{2}I_{nJ}|^{1/2}},$ 
 where the second line follows from standard marginalization properties of Gaussian-Gaussian models. 
 We now calculate the conditional posterior of the discrepancy function at the observed time points $\vec{t}$ . By Bayes theorem, we have 
 $\displaystyle\pi(\vec{\delta}_{\theta}|\vec{y},\theta,\sigma^{2},\phi)$ 
 $\displaystyle\propto\pi(\vec{y}|\vec{\delta}_{\theta},\theta)\pi(\delta_{\theta}|\theta,\phi)$ 
 (18) 
 $\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{N}_{Jn}(\vec{y};\vec{z}_{\theta}+\vec{\delta}_{\theta},\sigma^{2}I_{nJ})\mathcal{N}_{Jn}(\delta_{\theta};\vec{0},C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t}))$ 
 $\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{N}_{Jn}\left(\vec{\delta}_{\theta};\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Gamma_{\theta}^{-1}\left(\vec{y}-\vec{z}_{\theta}\right),\Gamma_{\theta}^{-1}\right)$ 
 where 
 $\Gamma_{\theta}=\left[C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t})\right]^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}I_{nJ},$ 
 which follows from the Conjugate properties of Gaussian-Gaussian models. Then the conditional posterior of the discrepancy is given by 
 $\pi(\vec{\delta}_{\theta}|\vec{y},\sigma^{2},\phi)\propto\int_{\Theta}\mathcal{N}_{Jn}\left(\vec{\delta}_{\theta};\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\Gamma_{\theta}^{-1}\left(\vec{y}-\vec{z}_{\theta}\right),\Gamma_{\theta}^{-1}\right)\pi(\theta|\vec{y},\sigma^{2},\phi)d\theta.$ 
 (19) 
 In general, eq. ( 19 ) will not have a closed form. However, it can be sample from via Monte-Carlo integration, where draws from eq. ( 17 ) are used to approximate the integral. 
 To construct a conditional posterior for the measurement error variance $\sigma^{2}$ , we use an inverse-gamma prior $\pi(\sigma^{2})=\operatorname{IG}(\sigma^{2};\alpha_{\sigma^{2}},\beta_{\sigma^{2}})$ . Then we have the closed form conditional posterior 
 $\displaystyle\pi(\sigma^{2}\mid\vec{y},\theta,\vec{\delta}_{\theta})$ 
 $\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{N}_{Jn}(\vec{y};\vec{z}_{\theta}+\vec{\delta}_{\theta},\sigma^{2}I_{nJ})\operatorname{IG}(\alpha_{\sigma^{2}},\beta_{\sigma^{2}})$ 
 (20) 
 $\displaystyle\propto\operatorname{IG}\left(\sigma^{2};\alpha_{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{Jn}{2},\beta_{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\left|\vec{y}-\vec{z}_{\theta}-\vec{\delta}_{\theta}\right|_{2}^{2}\right),$ 
 which follows from standard results in conjugacy. 
 For the unconstrained discrepancy kernel hyper-parameters $\phi$ , we assume independent uniform priors over problem-specific bounds, denoted collectively by $\pi(\phi)$ . Extending eq. ( 17 ), the joint conditional posterior of the calibration and kernel parameters is 
 $\displaystyle\pi(\theta,\phi\mid\vec{y},\sigma^{2})$ 
 $\displaystyle\propto\frac{\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}(\vec{y}-\vec{z}_{\theta})^{\intercal}\left(C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t})+\sigma^{2}I_{nJ}\right)^{-1}(\vec{y}-\vec{z}_{\theta})\right]\pi(\theta)\pi(\phi)}{\det\left|C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t})+\sigma^{2}I_{nJ}\right|^{1/2}}.$ 
 (21)

 4 Numerical Experiments 
 4.1 Experiment Design 
 4.1.1 Data Generation 
 We examine three underlying ODE models split into five sections. With slight abuse of notation, for each model type we use mathematical notation that is consistent with the modeling community. Thus, there may be overlap in the use of previously defined variables and each set of results should be interpreted within the context of the model domain. Each model is given specific upper and lower parameter bounds, denoted by UB and LB, respectively. For each model, we simulate observed data under observation model ( 1 ) where model parameters $\theta$ are sampled from box-uniform priors over the problem specific bounds and model discrepancy is simulated from the MOS prior outlined in Theorem  1 . This ensures that the simulated model parameters remain the $L^{2}$ minimizer according to Equation  8 and are the proper inferential target. The kernel hyperparameters $\phi$ are generated from a problem specific log-normal distribution, where we simultaneously infer $\phi$ in posterior inference to simulate the realistic setting where the discrepancy hyperparameters are unknown.
The measurement error is zero-mean Gaussian with a variance $\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}$ that is set based on the specific model system. Each experiment was repeated for $100$ Monte-Carlo replications and results combined for evaluation. 
 4.1.2 Implementation Details 
 All empirical results in this manuscript are generated using a custom Python implementation built on standard scientific computing libraries, including SciPy, NumPy, PyTorch, and GPyTorch, and made publicly available on Github ( https://github.com/mjcolebank/Discrepancy_USC ). Unless otherwise stated, default numerical and inferential settings are used throughout. The ODE system (with the state variables and sensitivites) are solved using SciPy’s odeint integration tool, which uses the LSODA algorithm [ 19 ] . Inputs ( $t$ ) correspond to the fixed simulation grid from the ODE solver and are in their physical units. Uniform priors defined using problem specific LB and UB are used for the model parameters. For each proposed parameter value, the simulator is solved on a fixed, prescribed time interval to provide the state and sensitivity outputs. Solving sensitivities directly avoids extra simulations and reduces numerical errors for gradient approximation. Sensitivity information is used only when building ( 14 ). Posterior inference is performed using Algorithm  1 for a single chain for 10,000 iterations. All inferences are conducted on the last 5,000 samples from the MCMC procedure, discarding the first 5,000 as burn-in. The initial proposal covariance is defined by the diagonal matrix $V^{0}=\textrm{diag}(\sigma_{\theta})$ , where $\sigma_{\theta}=0.01\left(\textrm{UB}-\textrm{LB}\right)$ , and updates are made every $k_{0}=2,000$ iterations. 
 4.1.3 Competing Methods 
 We compare our method to a multivariate extension of the Kennedy O’Hagan approach [ 23 ] (KOH), where the unconstrained discrepancy prior is modeled using a mean-zero independent multivariate Gaussian process [ 30 ] , and a radial basis function (RBF) for each output dimension, resulting in the Kronecker form 
 $\mathcal{C}_{\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t}^{\prime})=I_{J}\otimes C_{\phi}\left(\vec{t},\vec{t}^{\prime}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{Jn\times Jn},\ \ \ C_{\phi}(t_{i},t_{j})=\phi_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{(t_{i}-t_{j})^{2}}{2\phi_{2}^{2}}\right).$ 
 (22) 
 The kernel $C_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})$ is used in both the KOH and the MOS procedure, the latter of which uses $C_{\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t}^{\prime})$ to calculate $C_{\theta,\phi}(\vec{t},\vec{t}^{\prime})$ . Posterior inference using KOH was performed using the same Algorithm  1 , with settings kept largely consistent with those outlined in Section  4.1.2 , except replacing any computations involving orthogonalized kernel ( 15 ) with the kernel ( 22 ) for KOH. 
 4.1.4 Evaluation Metrics 
 We evaluate the estimation performance of the methods by computing the Monte-Carlo average of the root mean squared error (RMSE) and bias of the posterior mean. For UQ, we use the MCMC samples to form $95\%$ credible intervals and compute the empirical coverage and average interval length over the Monte-Carlo replications. 
 We also evaluate inference on the true process $\xi$ by sampling the push-forward of the posterior parameter and discrepancy samples through the discrepancy adjusted forward model. Estimation performance is quantified by approximating the integrated squared error and integrated bias of the posterior predicted process. The quality of the uncertainty quantification was assessed by constructing $95\%$ point-wise credible intervals over the time grid and computing their empirical coverage and average length using the Monte-Carlo replications. 
 4.2 Model 1: Mass-Spring-Damper Model 
 For our first example, we consider a coupled mass-spring-damper system given by the second-order, linear matrix differential equation 
 $\displaystyle{M}\ddot{x}+{C}\dot{x}+{K}{x}={F}.$ 
 (23) 
 Here, we relate the acceleration $\ddot{x}$ , velocity $\dot{x}$ , and position $x$ of masses that are connected to each other through a spring-dashpot system. Given $n_{s}$ springs, the above equation has four sets of parameters: a set of $n_{s}$ mass terms which are described by the diagonal matrix of masses ${M}$ , a set of $n_{s}$ dashpot elements (the dampers) parameterized by the bidiagonal matrix ${C}$ , and $n_{s}$ stiffness parameters given by the bidiagonal matrix ${K}$ . The vector ${F}$ includes the nonhomogenous component of the system, e.g., periodic forcing functions. We recast the system of $n_{s}$ second-order equations as a system of $2n_{s}$ first-order equations given by 
 $\frac{d}{dt}\begin{bmatrix}{x}\\
\dot{x}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{0}&{I}\\
-{M}^{-1}{K}&-{M}^{-1}{C}\ \ \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{x}\\
{\dot{x}}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0\\
F\end{bmatrix},$ 
 (24) 
 with known initial conditions $x(0)=x_{0}$ and $\dot{x}(0)=v_{0}$ . 
 Inferring the elements of ${M}$ , ${C}$ , ${K}$ , and ${F}$ simultaneously leads to non-identifiability, since the mass terms are not uniquely determined in this system [ 35 ] . Thus, we fix the masses ${M}$ and treat them as known quantities. We also leave the forcing terms ${F}$ fixed at their known value, and focus on inferring the parameters that make up the matrices ${C}$ and ${K}$ representing the dashpot and stiffness elements. 
 In general, the sensitivity system of eq. ( 24 ) can be derived by noting that, since the ODE system is linear in the states, the state-Jacobian is the evolution matrix 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{J{}}=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\
-M^{-1}K&-M^{-1}C\end{bmatrix}$ 
 (25) 
 which is true regardless of the number of springs. The term $\mathcal{G}_{\theta}$ also follows a pattern that is best seen on a case-by-case basis. 
 We begin with the case where there are $n_{s}=2$ masses, where the first mass is connected to a fixed surface and the second mass is free. The linear system is given by 
 $\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\begin{bmatrix}m_{1}&0\\[4.0pt]
0&m_{2}\\[4.0pt]
\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ \mathbf{K}=\begin{bmatrix}k_{1}+k_{2}&-k_{2}\\[4.0pt]
-k_{2}&k_{2}\\[4.0pt]
\end{bmatrix},\ \ \ \mathbf{C}=\begin{bmatrix}c_{1}+c_{2}&-c_{2}\\[4.0pt]
-c_{2}&c_{2}\\[4.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}.$ 
 (26) 
 Our parameter vector of interest is $\theta=\{k_{1},c_{1},k_{2},c_{2}\}$ . We assume that the forcing function is applied to the second mass only, giving 
 ${F}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\[4.0pt]
F_{0}\cos(\omega_{f}\cdot t)\\[4.0pt]
\end{bmatrix}$ 
 (27) 
 where $F_{0}$ is the forcing magnitude and $\omega_{f}$ is the forcing frequency. We fix the external force parameters for all simulations and data at $F_{0}=25$ and $\omega_{f}=5$ . The corresponding sensitivity system then uses $\mathcal{G}_{z}=\textrm{diag}\left(\mathcal{J}\right)$ defined in eq. ( 25 ), while the term $\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$ has the non-trivial elements 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{3}}=-x_{1}/m_{1},$ 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{7}}=-\dot{x}_{1}/m_{1}$ 
 (28) 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{11}}=(-x_{1}+x_{2})/m_{1},$ 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{12}}=(x_{1}-x_{2})/m_{2},$ 
 (29) 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{15}}=(-\dot{x}_{1}+\dot{x}_{2})/m_{1},$ 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{16}}=(\dot{x}_{1}-\dot{x}_{2})/m_{2}.$ 
 (30)

 4.4 Model 3: The FitzHugh-Nagumo model 
 In this section, we consider the FitzHugh–Nagumo model, which is an oscillator-like equation that can describe the dynamics of excitatory and inhibitory neurons. The system of equations is given by 
 $\displaystyle\frac{dv}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=v-\frac{v^{3}}{3}-w+I$ 
 (41) 
 $\displaystyle\frac{dw}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=(v+a-bw)\frac{1}{\tau}$ 
 (42) 
 where $v$ and $w$ represent the excitatory and inhibitory signals, respectively. The parameters are $a$ and $b$ , which control the threshold for excitatory spiking and the resiting potential, an external applied current $I$ , and the time-scale modulation $\tau$ , which controls how the inhibitory signal acts relative to the excitatory signal. Thus, our parameters for inference are $\theta=\left\{a,b,I,\tau\right\}$ . The sensitivity system is then controlled by $\mathcal{G}_{z}=\textrm{diag}\left(\mathcal{J}\right)$ , where 
 $\mathcal{J}=\begin{bmatrix}1-v^{2}&-1\\
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}&\displaystyle\frac{-b}{\tau}\end{bmatrix}$ 
 (43) 
 and the vector $\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{8}$ which has four non-trivial entries 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{3}}=1,$ 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{5}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau},$ 
 (44) 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{6}}=\frac{-1}{\tau},$ 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{8}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\frac{-(v+a-bw)}{\tau^{2}}.$ 
 (45)

 4.5 Model 4: The susceptible-infected-recovered-vaccinated (SIRV) disease model 
 The susceptible-infected-recovered-vaccinated (SIRV) model is a system of nonlinear ODEs given by 
 $\displaystyle\frac{dS}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=-\alpha SI/N-vS+{w_{r}}R+w_{v}V$ 
 (46) 
 $\displaystyle\frac{dI}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\alpha SI/N-\mu I$ 
 $\displaystyle\frac{dR}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\mu I-w_{r}R$ 
 $\displaystyle\frac{dV}{dt}$ 
 $\displaystyle=vS-w_{v}V$ 
 where $\alpha$ is the infection rate, $v$ is the vaccination rate, $\mu$ represents the transition from infected to recovered, $w_{r}$ is the rate of diminished immunity that leads to being susceptible again, and $w_{v}$ reflects the diminished effects of vaccination. The term $N$ is the total population size, where we assume $N=S+I+V+R$ is a constant. The state vector $(S(t),I(t),R(t),V(t))^{\intercal}$ denotes the susceptible, infected and recovered populations at time $t$ , respectively. Thus, the full parameter set is $\theta=\left\{\alpha,v,\mu,w_{r},w_{v}\right\}$ . These models have been used extensively to study the spread of pathogens such as measles, dengue virus, and, more recently, COVID-19 [ 16 , 20 , 39 ] . 
 The sensitivity system of the SIRV model can be derived with knowledge of $\mathcal{J}$ and $\mathcal{G}_{\theta}$ . The Jacobian, $\mathcal{J}$ is 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{J}=\begin{bmatrix}-\alpha I/N-v&-\alpha S/{N}&w_{r}&w_{v}\\[6.0pt]
\alpha I/{N}&\alpha S/{N}-\mu&0&0\\[6.0pt]
0&\mu&-w_{r}&0\\[6.0pt]
v&0&0&-w_{v}\end{bmatrix}$ 
 (47) 
 and $\mathcal{G}_{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{20}$ has nonzero entries 
 $\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{\theta_{1}}=-SI/{N},\ \ \mathcal{G}_{\theta_{2}}=-S,\ \ \mathcal{G}_{\theta_{4}}=R,\ \ \mathcal{G}_{\theta_{5}}=V,\ \ \mathcal{G}_{\theta_{6}}=SI/N,$

 4.5.1 Inference Results 
 We use 20 equally spaced time points on the interval $\Omega=[0,5]$ , representing five days. Each dataset is generated by sampling parameters from uniform distributions, with $a\sim\mathcal{U}(8,16)$ , $v\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,1)$ , $\mu\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,1)$ , $w_{r}\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,1)$ , and $w_{v}\sim\mathcal{U}(0.1,1)$ . The initial conditions for the model are $S(0)=0.99999$ , $I=0.00001$ , and $V(0)=R(0)=0$ . We add independent zero-mean Gaussian noise to each dataset, with a noise variance $\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2}=0.001$ . For the model discrepancy, we generate a signal using the MOS framework with hyperparameters $\gamma\sim\textrm{LogNormal}\left(-7,1.0^{2}\right)$ and $\ell\sim\textrm{LogNormal}\left(-0.5,0.8^{2}\right)$ . We use these priors for the GP to ensure a relatively small discrepancy magnitude (since the states are scaled by the population size) and a somewhat large length-scale that allows for oscillations slowly across the first few days of the simulation.

 5 Conclusion and Future Work 
 Model discrepancy is a significant source of uncertainty in the case of simulation models [ 17 , 24 , 31 ] , yet this form of uncertainty is sparsely considered in physics and biological models governed by complex ODE models. To address this gap, we provide a novel approach to multi-output ODE model calibration under model discrepancy that is demonstrated to improve upon the dominant existing approach in several key areas. Central to our approach is a new discrepancy prior, semi-automatically elicited from the sensitivity structure of the forward model, that extends the orthogonal-prior construction of [ 32 ] to multivariate ODE calibration. Extensive experimental evaluation demonstrates that our proposed MOS approach outperforms the standard method of multivariate calibration under model discrepancy (KOH). In particular, the MOS approach consistently results in lower estimation errors and largely provides well-calibrated intervals for (identifiable) model parameters and process predictions. This is anticipated, as the use of sensitivity-based priors on the discrepancy helps enforce a key structural identifiability condition on the target parameter. 
 Our approach is not without several limitations. First, this specific approach leverages analytical sensitivities for ODE models. This would be limited for systems with parameters and/or state variables that in very high dimensions. Deriving sensitivity equations can be timely and complex in this setting. In addition, the computational complexity of solving the sensitivity system simultaneously with the ODE can be burdensome with high-dimensional parameter vectors. Potential solutions to this, such as the use of automatic differentiation and Jacobian-vector products in packages like Jax [ 5 ] , should be investigated next. We generated data for our study from the models and MOS approach, hence our results are not for actual measurements that might suffer from even larger model discrepancy. Additional investigations into the selection of Gaussian process kernels is also warranted, as specific kernels may be better suited for certain simulations based on their sensitivity systems.

The zero mean condition of $\delta_{\theta}$ implies $f_{\theta}$ is mean zero and so the zero mean condition follows trivially. We now consider the variance.

 $\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\left(\int_{\Omega}S_{z}(t,\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}\delta_{\theta}(t)dt\right)\left(\int_{\Omega}S_{z}(t^{\prime},\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}\delta_{\theta}(t^{\prime})dt^{\prime}\right)^{\intercal}]$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}S_{z}(t,\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}\mathbb{E}[\delta_{\theta}(t)\delta_{\theta}^{\intercal}(t^{\prime})]AS_{z}(t^{\prime},\theta)dtdt^{\prime}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}S_{z}(t,\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}C_{\theta,\phi}(t,t^{\prime})AS_{z}(t^{\prime},\theta)dtdt^{\prime}$ 
 $\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}S_{z}(t,\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}C_{\phi}(t,t^{\prime})AS_{z}(t^{\prime},\theta)dtdt^{\prime}}_{H_{\theta}}-$ 
 $\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}S_{z}(t,\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}[h_{\theta}^{\intercal}(t)H_{\theta}^{-1}h_{\theta}(t^{\prime})]AS_{z}(t^{\prime},\theta)dtdt^{\prime}$ 
 $\displaystyle=H_{\theta}-\underbrace{[\int_{\Omega}S_{z}(t,\theta)^{\intercal}A^{\intercal}h_{\theta}^{\intercal}(t)dt]}_{H_{\theta}}H_{\theta}^{-1}\underbrace{[\int_{\Omega}h_{\theta}(t^{\prime})]AS_{z}(t^{\prime},\theta)dt^{\prime}]}_{H_{\theta}}$ 
 $\displaystyle=H_{\theta}-H_{\theta}H_{\theta}^{-1}H_{\theta}=0\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}$ 
 Hence, the $p$ -dimensional Gaussian random vector $\int f_{\theta}$ has zero mean and covariance, and thus equals zero with probability 1, as desired to show.
∎ 
 Appendix B Sensitivity Results 
 We calculated average sensitivity metrics by using the sensitivity systems defined for each model. We calculate a scalar index for the model sensitivity by first integrating the time series sensitivity to provide $\bar{S}_{i}=\int_{\Omega}S_{i}(t)dt$ for each dataset. We calculate the sensitivity at the data generating parameter. Then, we average over all 100 data instances to provide an average sensitivity, $\mathcal{S}_{i}=\frac{1}{100}\sum_{i=1}^{100}\bar{S}_{i}$ . We then provide plots of a scaled sensitivity, $\mathcal{S}^{*}_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}/\underset{i}{\max}{\mathcal{S}_{i}}$ to visualize the relative impact of the parameters on each state variable.
